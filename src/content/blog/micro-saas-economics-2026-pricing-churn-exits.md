Between May and October 2026, 592 solo founders filled in a form to get listed in The Startup Project's directory. Four in five had launched a product. Yet 82% reported no revenue at all, and 15 of them reported $1,000 or more a month ([The Startup Project, Nov 2026](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)).

Shipping is no longer the hard part. If you run a small software product alone, you have probably met what comes next: a low price that signs people up and then loses them, and AI features that cost money on every request.

This piece reads six primary sources published between April and November 2026 on revenue, pricing, churn and exits. Each source has its own sample, so every number stays with its survey.

## What 592 Solo Founders Report Earning

The Startup Project received 1,578 directory submissions in six months. It removed 236 repeat websites, 350 automated or bulk entries and 22 local service businesses, which left 970 startups. Of those, 592 (61%) listed a team size of one ([The Startup Project](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)).

Most of those solo founders had a live product: 80% described themselves as launched or further along, and only 5.4% used a free hosting subdomain. Two revenue figures in the report sit close together and mean different things. The 82% counts every solo founder who reported no revenue. The 63% is the narrower group who said they had launched and still had none ([The Startup Project](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)).

The top of the range is thin. Of the 592, 15 (2.5%) reported $1,000 or more a month, and four reported $10,000 or more. ([The Startup Project](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)).

Teams look different, and the report attaches a warning. In its chart, 48% of teams were pre-revenue and 30% reported $1,000 or more a month. The team group runs from two co-founders to companies of 50 or more, and "hiring people usually follows revenue," so the gap does not prove that a co-founder produces income ([The Startup Project](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)).

AI products did a little better, on small counts. Among solo founders, 46% described an AI product. Of those, 22% reported some revenue against 14% of the rest, and 12 of 275 reported $1,000 or more a month against 3 of 317. The report says these figures "do not show that adding AI to a product produces revenue" ([The Startup Project](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)).

Then came September. Filtered submissions ran between 91 and 138 a month from May to August, then hit 251 in September and 263 in October. The solo share rose from 55% to 66%, while the share of solo founders reporting $1,000 or more a month slipped from 3.2% to 2.1% ([The Startup Project](https://startupproject.org/resources/state-of-solo-founders/)). The publisher does not know what caused the jump.

## Why the Sub-$30 Tier Keeps Leaking

For founders who do reach revenue, ChartMogul's data points at price. Analyst Kyle Poyar looked at 1,043 SaaS and AI companies that reached $10,000 in monthly recurring revenue (MRR) on or before May 1, 2023 and still operate. He sorted them by average revenue per account (ARPA) at that point, using the animal names from investor Christoph Janz ([ChartMogul, June 2026](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

| Segment by monthly ARPA ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)) | Share at $10k MRR | Median GRR at $10k MRR | NRR at $10k MRR | NRR 3+ years later |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mouse, under $30 | 34% | 24.6% | 40.4% | 56.2% |
| Rabbit, $30–$299 | 53% | 31.6% | 68.1% | 76.7% |
| Deer, $300–$2,999 | 12% | 70.5% | not given | 87.4% |
| Elephant, $3,000 or more | 1% | 100% | not given | 93.4% |

Gross revenue retention (GRR) is the share of recurring revenue a company keeps from existing customers, before upgrades. Net revenue retention (NRR) counts upgrades too. Both figures are annualized. A median GRR of 24.6% means the typical under-$30 company was on pace to lose about three quarters of its starting revenue within a year ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

The pattern does not fade with scale. Three or more years later, the median company has grown about 20 times to $224k MRR. The under-$30 group still shows 56.2% NRR, against 87.4% for the $300 to $2,999 group. And 70% of companies still sell to the segment they started with ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

Moving is rare and usually goes one step up: 13% of the dataset went from Rabbit to Deer and 9% from Mouse to Rabbit. HubSpot is Poyar's example. It started with a flat fee of $250 a month and, 20 years later, averages almost exactly $1,000 per customer per month ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

Growth splits the same way. Companies that started in the Deer range and stayed there grow 22% year over year on average, against 2% for Mouse, 4% for Rabbit and 5% for Elephant. The report's headline calls that "3-5x faster," though the averages it lists put the gap between about four and 11 times. At $10k MRR, growth rates were nearly identical across the four groups ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

Direction matters too. Companies that moved up from Rabbit to Deer average roughly 9% growth. Those that moved down from Rabbit to Mouse average -11%, which Poyar labels correlation, not causation ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

## Growth that Fades, and the Pricing Levers that Stick

A second ChartMogul report follows what happens after the early climb. It covers more than 700 private software companies with at least $10k MRR, data going back to 2023 or earlier, and growth of at least 20% in 2024. Its measure is growth endurance: this year's growth rate divided by last year's ([ChartMogul, April 2026](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-growth-decay-report/)).

The median was 43%. The median company grew 65% in 2024 and 28% in 2025. Carried forward, that means 12% in 2026 and 5% in 2027 ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-growth-decay-report/)).

Speeding back up is uncommon. Only 18% of companies held or raised their growth rate. A startup growing 25% to 49% in 2024 had a one-in-25 chance of reaching 100% or more in 2025 ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-growth-decay-report/)).

The report then names companies that bent the curve, and one pricing change is small enough for a solo founder to copy. Predis, an AI ad generator, moved from an open free trial to one that requires a credit card on September 10, 2025. MRR growth spiked at once, and MRR has since tripled year over year. Fyxer had a similar result: free-to-paid conversion went from 5% to 35% after it added a credit card gate ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-growth-decay-report/)).

Predis co-founder Tanmay Ratnaparkhe explains why it worked: "Now we are able to offer more value to trial users (since we know they are serious)." Other cases in the report include Liveblocks, which relaunched pay-as-you-go pricing with a more transparent pricing page, and influencers.club, which moved from quarterly planning to weekly sprints ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-growth-decay-report/)). These are single-company accounts, not measured effects.

## The 50% Margin Problem Behind Hybrid AI Pricing

Growth Unhinged surveyed more than 230 B2B software and AI companies in April and May 2026. It is not a micro-SaaS sample: 22% had under $1M ARR and 25% had more than $150M. By product, 32% were mostly SaaS, 15% were AI-native and 43% were a hybrid of the two ([Growth Unhinged, May 2026](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)).

Hybrid pricing combines two or more models, such as a seat subscription with AI consumption on top. It is the most common model at 37%, up from 25% twelve months earlier. Small companies are the exception. Among those under $5M ARR, flat fees lead with 37% adoption ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)).

Margin is where AI changes the math. The median target gross margin for AI capabilities is about 50%, against 70% to 80% or more for SaaS. Only 12% aim for 80% or higher. When pricing AI, 54% named internal costs and margins as the most important factor, ahead of market or competitor pricing at 36% and productivity gains at 30% ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)).

Usage is lopsided. Poyar writes that in his experience the top 10% of power users drive more than 70% of token consumption. That is his observation, not a survey result ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)).

Credits are the common response. Today 29% of respondents have AI credits and another 33% plan to add them within six to 12 months. Poyar's caution is that credits "can become a nightmare for customers" ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026)).

## Metering Traps that Quietly Cap Usage

OpenMeter, a metering and billing product from Kong, read the same 230-company survey as a list of engineering requirements. Respondents expect flat fee and per seat pricing together to fall from 42% to 8% within three years, and outcome-based pricing to rise from 5% to 31% ([OpenMeter, Sept 2026](https://openmeter.io/blog/monetization-benchmarks-2026)).

OpenMeter's comparison shows what a 50% margin does to ordinary decisions ([OpenMeter](https://openmeter.io/blog/monetization-benchmarks-2026)):
- A 20% discount costs a quarter of profit at an 80% margin and 40% of profit at a 50% margin.
- A free tier stops being a marketing expense and becomes a real cost line.
- The heaviest user, close to free to serve at 80%, can be unprofitable alone at 50%.

The post's answer is cost attribution per customer, plus caps that act while a request is being served. "If your caps are a report you read after the invoice, you do not have caps. You have a postmortem," it says ([OpenMeter](https://openmeter.io/blog/monetization-benchmarks-2026)).

The quieter trap sits on the customer's side. Asked for their top two pricing challenges, 30% of outcome-priced companies and 20% of usage-priced companies said customers self-police usage and spend. People use less than they want because they do not trust the bill ([OpenMeter](https://openmeter.io/blog/monetization-benchmarks-2026)).

## What Buyers Pay for Small Profitable Apps

Acquire.com, a marketplace for buying and selling online businesses, published a recap of its State of the Deal 2026 webinar in September. It says it has helped close more than 5,000 deals worth over $1 billion since 2019 ([Acquire.com, Sept 2026](https://blog.acquire.com/business-acquisitions-2026-webinar/)).

The recap puts SaaS businesses at around 3–5x profit or 1–3x revenue, and agencies at around 1–3x profit or 1–2x revenue. It says the market "appears to have bottomed around 2024" ([Acquire.com](https://blog.acquire.com/business-acquisitions-2026-webinar/)).

Profit counts for more than size. "A smaller profitable business can attract more buyer interest than a larger company operating around breakeven," the recap says. Smaller businesses still draw serious interest, "particularly below the $150,000 range" ([Acquire.com](https://blog.acquire.com/business-acquisitions-2026-webinar/)).

Asking price decides how many buyers look. In the webinar data, pricing 20% above fair market value corresponded with about 2% serious buyer interest, and pricing 20% below it with about 92%. The recap does not say how many listings sit behind those two figures ([Acquire.com](https://blog.acquire.com/business-acquisitions-2026-webinar/)).

One listing reviewed live shows the ranges in use. An AI SEO SaaS business with nearly three years of history was priced at about 5x profit and 3.8x revenue. Past the multiple, buyers want predictable MRR, clean financials, documented standard operating procedures (SOPs) and a business that runs without its founder ([Acquire.com](https://blog.acquire.com/business-acquisitions-2026-webinar/)).

## What These Six Surveys Do Not Cover

None of the six samples is random, and none follows a single micro-SaaS from first dollar to sale. The Startup Project's revenue bands are self-reported and unchecked, and the report says it probably undercounts solo founders with steady income.

ChartMogul's two reports cover its own customers, and the retention study includes only companies that reached $10k MRR and survived three more years. The report says they "may not be representative of all SaaS and AI companies," and Poyar thinks the 34% Mouse share reflects ChartMogul's customer base more than the market ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-startups-become-what-they-hunt/)).

Growth Unhinged and OpenMeter describe one survey, not two, and this piece uses the public summary of a paywalled 37-page report. The two posts differ on one detail: Growth Unhinged says the share targeting margins of 20% or lower is similar to the 12% aiming for 80% or more, and OpenMeter puts it at 14% ([OpenMeter](https://openmeter.io/blog/monetization-benchmarks-2026)). OpenMeter sells metering.

Acquire.com's figures come from a webinar about its own marketplace, not from a published dataset. No source here ties a price point to an exit multiple, so the link between the two is an inference.

## Action Steps for Micro-SaaS Founders

### This week
- Attribute token and compute cost to each customer on your free plan and your cheapest paid plan. Add a usage cap that acts in real time and that the customer can see.
- Test one offer above the under-$30 range: a workflow-level plan at $100 to $300 or more a month, or a free trial that requires a credit card.

### This month
- Track cost per customer, GRR and NRR by three-month signup cohort, split by plan.
- Draft a hybrid plan with a base subscription plus a predictable credit allowance or usage cap. Clean up your books, write SOPs for recurring tasks and hand off one job that only you can do today.
